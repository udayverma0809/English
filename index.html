🧱 Uday

What is a sentence? Start here

📖 What is a sentence?

A sentence is a group of words that gives one complete message. It tells us who or what we are talking about and what is happening.

👤 Subject

The subject is the person, place or thing the sentence is about. Ask "Who or what?" before the verb. In "Meera sings", the subject is Meera.

⚙️ Predicate

The predicate tells what the subject does or is, and it always has a verb. In "Meera sings", the predicate is "sings".

🔤 Phrase vs clause

A phrase has no subject-verb pair, like "in the park". A clause has both, like "birds sing".

🧩 Independent vs dependent

An independent clause makes sense alone. A dependent clause starts with a word like because or when and leaves us waiting for more.

🎯 Why learn this?

Once you can find clauses, naming any sentence type is easy: just count the clauses and label each one.

🧱 Uday

Building blocks of a sentence

Every sentence is made of clauses. A clause needs a subject (who/what) and a predicate (what it does).

Phrase

Words with no subject + verb pair.

"after the lecture"

Independent clause (IC)

Subject + verb + complete thought.

The students laughed.

Dependent clause (DC)

Subject + verb, incomplete thought.

because the joke was good 🤔

🧠 Test: say it aloud. If you want to ask "...and then?", it is dependent.
🧱 Uday

Simple sentences in easy words

One idea

A simple sentence has only one subject-verb pair, so it carries one main idea. Think of it as a single thought.

Long but still simple

We can add describing words and place or time phrases. "The very tired students slept on the grass after lunch" is long, yet it has one clause.

Two subjects

Two subjects can share one verb: "Ravi and Sita laughed." Still simple, because there is only one clause.

Two verbs

One subject can do two actions: "She opened the door and walked in." Still one clause, so still simple.

When to use it

Use simple sentences for clear facts, instructions, headlines and strong, punchy points.

Common mistake

Do not call a sentence complex just because it is long. Count clauses, not words.

🧱 Uday

Rules: clauses & simple sentences

Description: A simple sentence expresses one complete idea using a single independent clause. It can contain phrases, modifiers, and compound subjects or verbs, but never a dependent clause.

1. Every clause needs a subject + finite verb

Birds fly.

2. An independent clause expresses a complete thought

The bell rang. ✅   When the bell rang ❌

3. A simple sentence = exactly 1 IC, 0 DC

The rain stopped.

4. Compound subject or verb stays simple

Tea and coffee are hot. / He ran and jumped.

5. No comma between subject and verb

❌ The tired students, slept. ✅ The tired students slept.

6. Capital letter at the start, . ? ! at the end

Did the team win? Yes!

🧱 Uday

Simple sentence

Formula: exactly one independent clause. No dependent clause at all.

Basic

The professor smiled.

Compound subject

Riya and Aman won.

Compound verb

We sang and danced all night.

Loaded with phrases

After class, the tired students slept under the old banyan tree.

💥 Myth buster: "simple" does NOT mean short. Long sentence, one clause = still simple.

🔗 Tejasvi

Compound sentences: the idea

🔗 Meaning

Compound means joined together. A compound sentence joins two complete sentences, each able to stand alone, into one.

🤝 Equal partners

Both parts are equally important. Neither depends on the other, like two friends walking side by side.

✨ Why join them?

Joining related ideas makes writing smooth. "I was hungry. I ate." becomes "I was hungry, so I ate."

🛠️ Three tools

Use a comma with a FANBOYS word, a semicolon, or a semicolon with a word like however followed by a comma.

💡 What the joiner means

And adds, but contrasts, or gives a choice, so shows a result, and for gives a reason.

✅ Quick check

Cover the joiner. If you can read two complete sentences on either side, it is compound.

🔗 Tejasvi

FANBOYS and joiners in easy language

Why a comma?

The comma tells the reader one full thought has ended and another is starting. Without it, the sentence can confuse.

The "and" trap

"Ravi sang and danced" has one subject, so no comma. "Ravi sang, and Sita danced" has two subjects, so we add a comma.

The word "nor"

Nor joins two negative ideas and flips the order: "He did not call, nor did he write."

Conjunctive adverbs

Words like however, therefore, moreover, otherwise and consequently also link clauses. Put a semicolon before and a comma after.

What is a semicolon?

It is stronger than a comma but softer than a full stop. Use it when two ideas are closely linked.

Choosing a joiner

Pick by meaning: add (and), contrast (but, yet), choice (or), reason (for), result (so).

🔗 Tejasvi

Rules: compound sentences

Description: A compound sentence joins two or more independent clauses of equal importance. The link shows addition, contrast, choice, reason or result.

1. Comma + FANBOYS joins two ICs

I studied, so I passed.

2. A semicolon can join closely related ICs

I studied; I passed.

3. Adverb joiner: semicolon before, comma after

I studied; therefore, I passed.

4. Both halves must stand alone

❌ I studied, and passed the test. (one subject: simple)

5. Never join ICs with a comma alone

❌ I studied, I passed. (comma splice)

6. Colon when clause 2 explains clause 1

He had one goal: he wanted to win.

🔗 Tejasvi

Compound sentence

Formula: independent clause + independent clause (equal partners 🤝)

1. Comma + FANBOYS

I wanted to sleep, but the deadline was tonight.

2. Semicolon

The exam ended; we celebrated.

3. Semicolon + adverb

I was late; however, I found a seat.

✂️ Test: swap the joiner for a full stop. If both halves still stand alone, it is compound.

🎛️ Vandit

Complex sentences: the idea

🎛️ Meaning

Complex means one idea has another idea attached to it. One clause is the main part and the other gives extra information.

👑 Main and helper

The independent clause can stand alone. The dependent clause cannot, so it needs the main clause to be complete.

❓ What does the helper tell us?

When? (when, after) · Why? (because) · Under what condition? (if, unless) · Despite what? (although).

🔑 Subordinators

These small words start a dependent clause: because, although, if, unless, when, while, since, until, after, before.

👥 Relative pronouns

Who, whom, whose, which and that start clauses that describe a noun, such as "the girl who sings".

✏️ Comma rule

If the helper comes first, put a comma after it. If it comes after the main clause, usually no comma.

🎛️ Vandit

Dependent clauses in easy language

🕐 Adverb clause

Works like an adverb and tells when, why or how. "We left after the rain stopped."

🎨 Adjective clause

Works like an adjective and describes a noun. "The teacher who helped me is kind."

📦 Noun clause

Works like a noun, as subject or object. "What you said surprised me."

➕ Essential or extra?

"Students who study pass" tells which students: no commas. "Rohan, who studies, passed" adds extra info: use commas.

🔍 Which type is it?

Ask what job the clause does: gives time or reason (adverb), describes a noun (adjective), or acts as a noun (noun clause).

⚠️ Fragment alert

A dependent clause alone is incomplete. "Because it rained." needs a main clause: "Because it rained, we stayed in."

🎛️ Vandit

Rules: complex sentences

Description: A complex sentence has one independent clause and at least one dependent clause. The ideas are unequal: the dependent clause adds time, reason, condition, contrast or extra detail.

1. DC begins with a subordinator or relative pronoun

because, although, if, when / who, which, that

2. DC before IC: comma after the DC

Although it rained, we played.

3. DC after IC: usually no comma

We played although it rained.

4. Non-essential relative clause: use commas

My uncle, who lives in Delhi, is a doctor.

5. Essential relative clause: no commas

The man who called is my uncle.

6. A DC alone is a fragment

❌ Because I was tired.

🎛️ Vandit

Complex sentence

Formula: 1 independent clause (the boss 👑) + 1 or more dependent clauses.

Clue words (subordinators)

because · although · if · unless · when · while · since · until · after · before · who · which · that

Dependent first → comma

Although it rained, we played cricket.

Dependent last → no comma

We played cricket although it rained.

🤹 Not equal partners like compound: here one clause depends on the other.
🎛️ Vandit

3 kinds of dependent clause

🕐 Adverb clause

Says when, why, how, where, on what condition.

When the bell rang, we ran.

🎨 Adjective clause

Describes a noun: who, which, that.

The book that you lent me is great.

📦 Noun clause

Acts as subject or object: that, what, whether.

I know that she won.

🧪 Trick: if "it" or "something" can replace the clause, it is a noun clause.

🚀 Vaidehi

Compound-complex: the idea

🚀 Meaning

It mixes both types: two or more complete sentences joined together, plus at least one extra helper clause.

🪜 Build it step by step

Simple: "We left." Compound: "We left, and they stayed." Add a helper: "Because it was late, we left, and they stayed."

🎯 Why use it?

It explains several connected ideas in one smooth sentence, which is very useful in essays and reports.

🔢 Counting rule

Two independent clauses plus one dependent clause is the minimum. More is possible but harder to read.

✏️ Punctuation

Comma after an opening helper clause, and a comma before the FANBOYS word that joins the two main clauses.

⚠️ Caution

Do not stuff in too many ideas. If you run out of breath reading it aloud, split it into two sentences.

🚀 Vaidehi

Rules: compound-complex sentences

Description: A compound-complex sentence has at least two independent clauses and at least one dependent clause. It links several related ideas with both equal and unequal relationships.

1. Minimum: 2 ICs + 1 DC

IC, and IC, because DC.

2. Join ICs with FANBOYS or a semicolon

We left; they stayed.

3. Attach DCs with subordinators

...because it was late.

4. Comma after an introductory DC

When it ended, we left, and they stayed.

5. Comma before the FANBOYS that joins the ICs

We left early, but they stayed.

6. Keep it readable: 3 to 4 clauses at most

Too many clauses confuse the reader.

🚀 Vaidehi

Compound-complex sentence

Formula: 2 or more independent + 1 or more dependent clauses. The combo meal 🍔

Because it was late, we left early, and the others stayed.

I like tea, but my sister, who lives abroad, prefers coffee.

🔢 Count clauses and tag each IC or DC. 2 ICs + 1 DC = compound-complex. Ideal for academic writing: rich ideas, tight logic.
🚀 Vaidehi

Analyse any sentence in 4 steps

1️⃣ Verbs

Find every verb.

2️⃣ Clauses

Pair each verb with its subject.

3️⃣ Label

IC or DC? Look for clue words.

4️⃣ Count

1 IC = simple · 2+ IC = compound · IC + DC = complex · both = compound-complex

Worked example

Because it was late, we left, and they stayed.

DC + IC + IC → compound-complex ✅

🚀 Vaidehi

Telling the four types apart: easy recipe

1️⃣ Count clauses

Only one clause means simple. Do not count phrases, only subject-verb pairs.

2️⃣ Two or more main clauses?

Look for FANBOYS or semicolons. If found, it is compound or compound-complex.

3️⃣ Helper clause present?

Look for because, although, if, when, who, which, that. If yes, it is complex or compound-complex.

4️⃣ Both present?

A joiner of equals plus a helper clause means compound-complex.

📝 Practice

"Although he tried, he failed, but he learned." One helper + two main clauses = compound-complex.

🧠 Remember

Simple 1 · Compound 2 equal · Complex main + helper · Compound-complex both.

🎮 Vinay

Sentence errors in easy language

❌ Fragment

A piece that looks like a sentence but is incomplete because it has no main clause. Fix: attach it to a full sentence.

❌ Comma splice

Two full sentences stuck together with only a comma, which is too weak. Fix: full stop, semicolon, or comma + FANBOYS.

❌ Run-on

Two full sentences with nothing between them. Fix: add punctuation or a joining word.

🔍 How to spot them

Break the sentence at each verb. Does each part have a subject and a complete thought, and are they joined properly?

🎓 Why it matters

Broken sentences cost marks and confuse readers. Correct ones make your meaning clear and your writing professional.

🛠️ Three fixes

Use a full stop, a semicolon, or a joiner. These three tools repair almost every sentence error.

🎮 Vinay

Variety and style in easy language

🎵 Why vary?

Only short sentences sound jumpy. Only long ones tire the reader. A mix keeps people interested.

💥 Simple = power

A short sentence after long ones grabs attention. "It worked."

⚖️ Compound = balance

Use it to show two ideas of equal weight, like advantages and disadvantages.

🧠 Complex = reasoning

Use it to show why, when or despite what. Great for explanations and arguments.

🏛️ Compound-complex = depth

Use it sparingly for rich ideas in essays and reports.

📚 Practice habit

Read your own writing aloud and mark each sentence type. Aim for a healthy mix.

🎮 Vinay

Rules: fixing errors & variety

Description: Errors in clause boundaries make writing unclear. Learning to repair fragments, comma splices and run-ons, and mixing sentence types, makes writing correct and engaging.

1. Fragment: add an IC or attach the DC

Because I was tired, I left.

2. Comma splice: period, semicolon, or comma + FANBOYS

It rained; we stayed in.

3. Run-on: add punctuation or a joiner

I love music, and I play daily.

4. Vary sentence openings and lengths

Short. Then a longer one that flows.

5. Match type to purpose

Simple = emphasis · Compound = balance · Complex = logic

6. Read aloud to catch long or broken sentences

If you run out of breath, split it.

🎮 Vinay

Spot the bug 🐛

Fragment

❌ Because I was tired.

✅ Because I was tired, I left.

No independent clause.

Comma splice

❌ It rained, we stayed in.

✅ It rained, so we stayed in.

A comma alone cannot join two ICs.

Run-on

❌ I love music I play daily.

✅ I love music; I play daily.

No joiner or punctuation.

🏆 These three mistakes cost the most marks in college writing.
